Engineered timber: structure that stores carbon

A school that opens sooner. A structure with a smaller carbon footprint. A building programme that can repeat what works. Engineered timber brings the precision of manufacturing to construction: its value begins with the material and comes to life when the whole building is designed around it.

The Technology

Wood, engineered to carry a building

Engineered timber is sawn wood glued into large, predictable structural elements: cross-laminated timber (CLT) for walls, floors and roofs, glue-laminated timber (glulam) for beams and columns. Combined with prefabrication, it turns a natural material into an industrial building system.

How It’s Built

From forest to finished structure

Boards are graded and glued crosswise into panels of 3, 5, 7 or more layers, pressed, then cut by machine to the design: up to about 18 m long and generally up to 300 mm thick. On site the panels are assembled dry. At Brock Commons in Vancouver, nine installers assembled an 18-storey superstructure and its exterior panels in 70 days.

Benefits

What engineered timber offers a project

Eight benefits, roughly in the order a public client weighs them. Where the evidence is limited, we say so; each benefit carries its source.

  1. Prefabrication moves decisions forward, so the site assembles what the team has already resolved. Panels arrive in sequence and are connected dry. At Brock Commons nine installers assembled an 18-storey superstructure in 70 days; a US redesign study reports savings of up to 3.5 months on high-rise structures.

    Source: Brock Commons Source: LeMessurier et al.

  2. Over its whole life a timber frame carries less embodied carbon than a concrete or steel frame, though the results overlap and depend on design. Wood also holds the carbon the tree absorbed for as long as the building stays in use, if the forests are sustainably managed.

    Source: Hart et al. Source: Churkina et al.

  3. A timber frame weighs a fraction of the equivalent concrete frame. Less weight can mean lighter foundations and fewer heavy deliveries, depending on the site.

    Source: Hart et al.

  4. A light structure attracts smaller seismic forces. A seven-storey CLT building was shaken with the 1995 Kobe earthquake record on the world’s largest shaking table without significant damage; the authors note relatively high accelerations in the upper storeys as a point for further research.

    Source: SOFIE project

  5. Boards are graded and checked for moisture, glued under controlled conditions and cut by machine for windows, doors and stairs. Panels reach the site as finished elements.

    Source: GreenSpec

  6. Dry assembly means little cement mixing, heavy plant and dust on site, and fewer truck movements through the neighbourhood. The evidence is indicative: it comes from industry guidance and project accounts, not independent studies.

    Source: Construction Canada

  7. A systematic review of nine randomized trials found that longer exposure to visible wood tended to improve mood and lower physiological arousal, though the results are not fully consistent. Timber’s thermal mass can also moderate indoor temperature, as two years of monitoring in a five-storey CLT hotel suggested; the benefit depends on climate and design.

    Source: systematic review Source: ORNL

  8. Engineered timber is no longer experimental: Brock Commons in Vancouver has 18 storeys, HoHo Wien in Vienna 24 storeys and 84 m, and Austria alone produced almost 700,000 m³ of CLT in 2024.

    Source: HoHo Wien Source: Holzkurier / Eurostat
